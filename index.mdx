We work with organisations and projects wherever a computer is, or should be, part of how the
work gets done — high tech when that's needed, low tech when that's what's right.

Sometimes that means building something that isn't there yet. More often it's getting more out
of what you already run.

> "Simplicity — the art of maximizing the amount of work not done — is essential."
> — Principle 10, Agile Manifesto

## What that looks like

Some of what we've built, from two-day jobs to systems running for years:

- Giving project managers their own cost and revenue numbers, live, out of an accounting system
  that had no way to share them safely.
- Posting supplier invoices straight into the ERP, so nobody keys them through a portal first.
- Working out which project a machine was on from where it actually was, rather than from a
  form filled in afterwards.
- Reading every note from a week of field inspections, and emailing management what mattered.
- Getting machine telemetry and GPS into the system crews already have open, instead of a
  portal only head office logs into.
- Texting someone the moment their timesheet looks wrong, while they can still remember the
  shift.
- Building a sales pipeline dashboard the CRM wanted a plan upgrade for, without standing up a
  database to do it.
- Letting payroll and project costing read the same timesheet, instead of asking staff to file
  two.
- Tagging physical assets so the code on the tag still resolves after the systems behind it
  change.

## How we work

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Card title="Right-sized, not one-size">
    Sometimes the answer is a process change, a spreadsheet, or not automating at all. And what
    we leave out counts too — keeping card numbers, personal details and corporate secrets
    outside what we build is why the rest doesn't need fort-knox security.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The best software isn’t always the best fit">
    Best-in-class doesn't help if the pricing model breaks as you add people, or your own data
    sits behind an upgrade. We push what you already run to its limits first, and when something
    new is needed we weigh how it prices and what it will let you reach — not just its feature
    list.
  </Card>
  <Card title="No two setups are the same">
    We arrive with no preferred stack and no fixed way of working. Some clients have an in-house
    tech team, some have a data office, some have neither. We work with yours, bring our own
    developers, or use a shared setup we run ourselves — and when something genuinely needs full
    IT-project scope, we help hand it to a proper dev house.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Organised data pays for itself">
    The unglamorous work — getting data out of the systems holding it and into a real database at
    the centre — is where the leverage is. It turns a report someone has to remember to open into
    an alert that finds them, or an action that just happens. And it's the platform anything
    later gets built on, AI included.
  </Card>
</Columns>

## How we use AI

We build with AI every day. Given the choice between organised data with no AI, and AI on top of
a mess, we'd take the organised data every time.

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Card title="AI for the exceptions, not the norm">
    The standard path should be plain deterministic code. AI earns its place on the cases that
    don't fit the pattern — cheaper to run, and more predictable where predictability matters.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Tokens to build, not to run">
    We'll spend tokens writing software; we'd rather not spend them every time it executes.
    Building this way is cheap enough that small automations now clear the bar — and once
    written, they're ordinary code with no AI cost per run.
  </Card>
</Columns>

Alongside IoT, comms, databases, data visualisation and advanced analytics.
